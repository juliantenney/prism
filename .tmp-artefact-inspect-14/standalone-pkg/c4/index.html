Overview

This self-study resource builds on the provisional library recommendation you recorded in c3. Retrieve that recommendation and its justification before beginning. You will examine subsequent evidence, compare the three available options, reconsider your judgement and identify what further information could warrant another change. Work through the four activities in order, using your earlier responses as the basis for reconsideration.

Learning purpose

The activities progress from analysing the effects of new evidence on your original reasoning, through comparing the available alternatives, to defending a reconsidered recommendation and identifying conditions for future revision. Your original recommendation remains the reference point throughout. The final activity applies the same reasoning process to a different university decision.

Knowledge summary

A provisional decision is a judgement supported by the evidence currently available but open to reconsideration when consequential information emerges. Reconsideration does not require a change of recommendation. It requires examining whether new findings strengthen, weaken, refine or leave unchanged the assumptions and interpretations supporting the original judgement.

Evidence and inference are different. The library's voluntary survey received 240 responses from approximately 12,000 students, with 168 respondents supporting extended opening hours. Those responses establish what participating students reported, but possible self-selection limits generalisation to the whole student population. Historical building-access records showed relatively low late-evening occupancy under previous arrangements. A later review found that this occupancy was concentrated on particular high-demand dates. Both observations can be accurate; neither establishes how students would use the library under substantially extended hours.

Different findings illuminate different considerations. Reports that late-evening users disproportionately experienced difficulty finding suitable study space elsewhere strengthen the relevance of access concerns for those users, without establishing how widespread the difficulty is across all students. Revised estimates indicate that targeted extensions would cost materially less than extending hours throughout the examination period. This informs the operational comparison but does not prove that targeted provision would meet future demand.

Comparative judgement requires weighing alternatives. Option A maintains existing hours, Option B extends hours throughout the examination period, and Option C targets extensions to anticipated high-demand dates. Each involves potential benefits, limitations and trade-offs. The combined evidence can change their relative merits without identifying one necessarily correct option.

Evidence-responsive reconsideration is proportionate. Maintaining, qualifying or revising an original recommendation can each be justified. Confirmation bias can arise when challenging evidence is selectively discounted; overreaction can arise when recent findings are treated as more decisive than their limitations permit. A defensible judgement considers both supporting and challenging evidence and distinguishes the preferred option from confidence in that preference.

Uncertainty can be made actionable. A recommendation can remain defensible while important questions remain unresolved. Identifying a consequential uncertainty, the further evidence needed to address it and the plausible findings that could affect the recommendation or confidence establishes meaningful conditions for future revision.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  1. Identify and explain how consequential new evidence affects specific assumptions, interpretations and uncertainties underpinning their own retained provisional library decision from c3. (LO1)
  2. Evaluate how the combined original and new evidence affects the relative merits of Options A, B and C, distinguishing justified inferences from conclusions that exceed the available evidence. (LO2)
  3. Justify a decision to maintain, qualify or revise their original recommendation, calibrating confidence in proportion to the combined evidence and distinguishing their reasoning from confirmation bias and overreaction to recent findings. (LO3)
  4. Identify consequential uncertainties that remain unresolved and specify further evidence that could justify a future change in their recommendation or confidence. (LO4)

Diagnostic Feedback: Consistency Without Rigidity

Review how you reconsidered

If your conclusion largely repeats your c3 justification: Check whether you have explicitly addressed findings that could challenge it. An unchanged recommendation can be defensible, but simply restating the original reasons may indicate confirmation bias: giving preferential attention to evidence that supports an existing position while discounting contrary information. Identify the strongest relevant new challenge and explain why it does or does not change the balance.

If you changed your recommendation as soon as you saw the follow-up findings: Check whether the new evidence warrants the strength of your conclusion. Overreaction occurs when recent findings are treated as more decisive than their limitations permit. Historical concentration of use, reports from a particular group of users and revised comparative costs can inform a decision without proving what will happen under new arrangements.

If you considered only evidence supporting your final preference: Return to the three-option comparison. Explain a credible reason someone might favour another option and identify the trade-off that leads you to weigh the evidence differently. The point is not to make every option appear equally persuasive, but to test whether your preference survives a fair comparison.

If you are uncertain how to express confidence: Separate your choice from your certainty. You might judge one option preferable while remaining uncertain about future use. Alternatively, new evidence may clarify a particular trade-off without changing your selected option. State what has become better supported and what remains unresolved.

Maintaining, qualifying and revising are all legitimate responses when they follow from balanced reasoning. A proportionate judgement is neither an automatic defence of the first decision nor an automatic reversal.

Simulated Transfer Case: Academic Support Sessions

A different university decision

Simulated evidence — fictional transfer scenario

A university department is considering whether to provide additional staffed academic support sessions during a concentrated assessment period. Staff time and room availability are limited, and the department must decide how much additional support to offer.

Three alternatives are available:

  • Option 1 — Retain current provision: Continue the existing schedule of staffed support sessions.
  • Option 2 — Extend throughout the assessment period: Offer additional staffed sessions across the full assessment period.
  • Option 3 — Target anticipated high-demand times: Offer additional staffed sessions at selected times when demand is expected to be greatest.

Original decision context

Initial indications of demand were limited. Existing session-use records did not indicate consistently high attendance, and extending staffed provision would require additional staff and room resources.

Provisional recommendation recorded by the department: Retain current provision for the time being.

Original justification: The limited evidence of sustained demand did not clearly establish that the additional resources required for extended sessions would be warranted.

Later development

After that recommendation, a closer review of previous assessment-period records found that use of staffed support sessions had been concentrated on particular dates rather than spread evenly across the period.

An updated operational comparison also found that targeted additional sessions would require fewer staff and room resources than extending sessions throughout the whole period.

What remains unknown

These findings describe previous use and comparative resource requirements. They do not establish how many students would attend if the department offered additional sessions or whether demand would occur at the same times in a future assessment period.

This case is separate from the library decision. Use it to test whether you can apply the same approach to a new provisional judgement without assuming that the new evidence determines a single correct outcome.

The transferable skill is the ability to adjust a judgement to the significance of evidence while remaining appropriately cautious about what is still unknown.

What to look for
