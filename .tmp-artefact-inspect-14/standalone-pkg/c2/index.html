Overview

This self-study resource uses a fictional university library scenario to explore how evidence can be evaluated and compared. Begin with the four evidence records in the first activity, then compare their contributions to different questions before producing a qualified synthesis. The activities take approximately 25 minutes and build on your earlier work distinguishing evidence, interpretation and assumption. You do not need to retrieve previous responses.

Learning purpose

The activities progress from evaluating individual sources to making comparative judgements and then integrating evidence into a qualified account. The first activity provides explicit criteria and a worked example; the later activities require increasingly independent reasoning. The opening-hours options provide a common context, but choosing between them belongs to the next learning component.

Knowledge summary

Evidence evaluation begins by distinguishing what a source reports from what someone infers from it. An observation may support a narrow conclusion without establishing a broader claim.

Relevance concerns whether evidence addresses the question being asked. Credibility concerns the grounds for trusting the source and its account. Reliability concerns the dependability of the process that produced the information. Limitations identify conditions or missing information that restrict what can reasonably be concluded.

Evidential strength describes how well a source supports a particular claim. Evidential significance describes how much that supported claim contributes to a particular question. Neither is a permanent ranking attached to a source independently of its use.

The fictional library evidence illustrates these distinctions:

  • A voluntary survey records that 168 of 240 respondents favoured extended hours, from a student population of approximately 12,000. It provides evidence about respondents' expressed preferences, but voluntary participation prevents an unsupported generalisation to all students. Expressed preferences do not establish future attendance.
  • Historical building-access records indicate relatively low late-evening occupancy under previous arrangements. They describe observed use in those conditions but do not establish demand for hours that were not previously available.
  • A small number of student comments report unequal access to quiet study facilities. They identify concerns worth considering but do not establish how widespread those concerns are. Limited evidence of prevalence does not make the reported concern unimportant.
  • Provisional operational costs identify additional staffing, security and operating implications. Their significance depends on the question being considered, and their provisional status means they must not be presented as fixed costs.

These sources address different phenomena: expressed preference, observed behaviour, reported access concerns and estimated resource implications. They may complement one another without corroborating the same claim. Apparent tension between survey support for extended hours and low historical occupancy is not necessarily a genuine contradiction, because the records concern different observations and conditions.

A defensible synthesis identifies each source's contribution, explains the limits of any connections between sources and preserves consequential uncertainty. It does not resolve missing relationships by assumption or treat uncertainty as a reason to discard every source. The resulting evaluation provides a basis for later consideration of the three opening-hours options; it does not itself determine which option should be chosen.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  1. Evaluate the relevance, credibility, reliability and limitations of individual evidence sources, explaining what each source can reasonably support and where broader conclusions would be unjustified. (LO1)
  2. Compare the evidential strength and significance of different sources, justifying how their contributions vary according to the question being considered rather than applying a single ranking criterion. (LO2)
  3. Synthesize the available evidence into a qualified evaluation that distinguishes apparent conflicts from genuine contradictions, identifies consequential uncertainties, and explains which conclusions remain supported without selecting a course of action. (LO3)

Five Ways to Evaluate Evidence

What can this evidence support?

Relevance concerns whether information addresses the particular claim being considered. A trustworthy record can still be irrelevant to a different question.

Credibility concerns reasons to trust the source and its account, including how information was obtained and represented. Credibility does not guarantee that every interpretation of the information is justified.

Reliability concerns the dependability of the information-producing process. A consistently recorded observation may be reliable for what was measured without predicting what would happen under different conditions.

Limitations identify what the evidence leaves unknown or what restricts its use. A limitation does not necessarily make evidence worthless; it may narrow the conclusion that can reasonably follow.

Evidential strength is the degree of support a source provides for a specific claim. To judge strength, connect the claim to the reported particulars, consider their credibility and reliability, and explain how limitations affect the conclusion. The same source may strongly support one claim and weakly support another.

Examine the brief explanation of relevance, credibility, reliability and evidential strength, including a separate illustration of how a limitation changes a conclusion.

Worked Example: Evaluating a Service Record

From an observation to a qualified judgement

Consider a separate fictional university service. A campus equipment-loan desk recorded 36 laptop loans on one Tuesday. Its electronic transaction log lists the date, each completed loan and the equipment category. A manager proposes the claim: Students need at least 36 loan laptops every day.

The transaction log is relevant because it records actual completed loans, rather than opinions about the service. Its credibility is supported by identifiable transaction records generated during normal service. Its reliability is plausible for counting completed loans, although the example provides no independent audit of the log. These features give reasonable grounds to accept that 36 loans were recorded on the specified Tuesday.

The proposed claim, however, concerns every day, while the observation concerns only one Tuesday. Completed loans also do not reveal requests that could not be met or how many students might have borrowed equipment under different arrangements. An alternative interpretation is that Tuesday's demand differed from other days. Neither interpretation can be resolved from this single record.

The record therefore provides stronger support for a day-specific account of completed borrowing than for a general daily requirement. The defensible judgement is: The desk recorded 36 completed laptop loans on the Tuesday observed; further evidence would be needed to estimate a typical daily requirement.

Notice the reasoning sequence: identify the precise claim, assess what was recorded, weigh the grounds for confidence against the observation's limits, and narrow the conclusion accordingly. Apply that method independently to the library evidence.

How Evidential Significance Changes

Strength is not the same as significance

Evidential strength concerns how well information supports a particular claim. Evidential significance concerns how much that supported claim contributes to the question under consideration.

Imagine two records about a university service: a precisely counted number of visits and a detailed account of an access difficulty. If the question concerns recorded attendance, the count may contribute more directly. If the question concerns barriers to participation, the account may be more pertinent, although it cannot establish how common the difficulty is. Neither source becomes universally superior.

The relationship is therefore: question → relevant claim → evidential support → significance for that question. Ranking every source by numerical precision alone ignores what each measures. Sources can also complement one another when they address different aspects of a situation; different findings are not necessarily contradictory.

Connect your learning

The final challenge is to bring the evidence together without forcing it into one ranking or moving prematurely to an opening-hours decision. You will produce a concise, qualified synthesis and then apply the same evaluative approach to a less familiar claim about the evidence.

A balanced synthesis should preserve what each source contributes, explain where sources address different questions, and identify uncertainties that genuinely restrict broader conclusions. Do not treat uncertainty as proof that the evidence is useless. Equally, do not resolve uncertainty by inventing relationships between sources. Your conclusion should concern what the evidence supports, not which action the university should take.
