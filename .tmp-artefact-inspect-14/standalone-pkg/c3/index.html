Overview

This self-directed resource uses a fictional university library decision to explore how evaluated evidence can inform a justified action. Begin by comparing three opening-hours policies, then select and defend a provisional recommendation. Finally, examine the assumptions and uncertainties affecting your confidence and retain your original reasoning for later reconsideration. The activities take approximately 30 minutes.

Learning purpose

The three activities form a connected progression: compare alternatives, justify a provisional choice, and qualify that choice. Your comparison provides the basis for your recommendation; your recommendation then becomes the subject of your uncertainty and confidence review. Use your own responses throughout. Earlier work is not transferred automatically.

Knowledge summary

A justified policy decision connects evidence, interpretation, priorities and comparison to a clearly stated action. Evidence establishes particular observations; interpretation considers what those observations may imply for possible actions. Assumptions identify conditions on which reasoning depends but which the available evidence does not establish.

In the fictional university library case, the available actions are Option A: maintain existing opening hours, Option B: extend opening hours throughout the examination period, and Option C: introduce targeted extensions during periods of greatest anticipated demand. Each option must be considered against student access needs, equity, staffing and operational implications, competing priorities and unresolved uncertainty.

The four original evidence sources support different, limited claims. A voluntary survey recorded 168 supporters of extended hours among 240 respondents, drawn from approximately 12,000 students. The 70% figure describes respondents, not the student population, and voluntary participation limits generalisation. Historical building-access records indicate relatively low late-evening occupancy under previous arrangements, but do not establish demand under extended opening hours. Limited student comments identify unequal access to suitable study space without establishing its prevalence. Staffing, security and operational costs are provisional, so their precise amounts and comparative implications remain unresolved.

Comparative reasoning examines what each action might achieve, what it may require and what could be forgone. A trade-off connects competing consequences rather than treating an isolated advantage as decisive. Evidence can inform these comparisons, but it does not independently determine how much weight to assign to access, equity, resource commitments or uncertainty.

A provisional recommendation selects one action and explains why it is preferable to both alternatives under explicitly stated priorities. Its strength depends on accurate evidence use, meaningful comparison and acknowledgement of accepted trade-offs. No single option is predetermined as correct: different choices may be defensible when their reasoning is transparent and proportionate to the available evidence.

Confidence concerns the strength of the evidence and reasoning supporting the recommendation, not certainty that the policy will succeed. Consequential assumptions and unresolved uncertainties should therefore be identified and connected to the judgement. Retaining the original action, reasons, priorities, assumptions and confidence makes subsequent reconsideration possible without retrospectively changing the initial decision.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  1. Compare the three established library opening-hours options using evaluated evidence to explain their relative advantages, disadvantages and trade-offs in relation to student access needs, operational costs and competing priorities. (LO1)
  2. Select and justify a provisional library opening-hours policy by connecting the chosen action to relevant evidence, explicitly weighted priorities and a reasoned comparison with both alternatives. (LO2)
  3. Qualify the provisional recommendation by identifying consequential assumptions, explaining unresolved uncertainties and justifying a degree of confidence proportionate to the available evidence and reasoning. (LO3)

From Evidence Evaluation to Policy Comparison

Comparing actions using evaluated evidence

Evaluating evidence asks whether a source is credible, relevant and sufficiently informative for a particular claim. Comparing actions asks a different question: what would each possible decision mean, given the evidence and its limitations?

A source can be useful without settling the decision. Evidence of student preferences may matter when considering access, while historical use may matter when considering likely demand. Neither automatically determines the best opening-hours policy. Operational commitments also matter, even when their precise costs remain provisional.

Dimensions of comparison

Student access and equity. Consider whether an option could affect students' opportunities to use suitable study space. Distinguish potential benefits from benefits that have been demonstrated.

Demand and use. Consider what is known about previous library use and what remains unknown about use under different opening arrangements.

Staffing and operations. Consider what an option may require in staffing, security and other resources. Avoid treating provisional costs as confirmed figures.

Consequences and trade-offs. A policy can offer a potential advantage while also creating a disadvantage or resource commitment. A trade-off explains why these competing consequences matter together.

Priorities. Evidence helps establish what is known, but it does not assign the importance of access, equity, resource use or uncertainty on your behalf. Different priorities can support different defensible recommendations.

A useful reasoning distinction

An observation reports something recorded or supplied in the case. An inference explains what that observation might imply for an option. A trade-off explains what may be gained, forgone or placed at risk when acting on that inference.

Keep these stages visible in your comparison. You do not need to calculate a score or identify a winning option yet.

Reasoning Towards a Defensible Recommendation

Turning comparison into justified action

The question is not simply whether students would welcome longer opening hours. It is which of the three available policies can be defended most convincingly under your stated priorities, given what the evidence does and does not establish.

A recommendation requires a judgement about competing considerations. The original evidence offers several relevant grounds for that judgement, but none determines a unique answer.

The survey records support for extended hours among the students who responded. That is relevant to potential access benefits, but voluntary participation means the result cannot be treated as a representative measure of demand across the university. Historical access records provide a different perspective: late-evening occupancy was relatively low under previous arrangements. They matter when considering resource commitments, but they do not establish how students would behave if opening arrangements changed. The limited student comments identify access difficulties that deserve consideration, without revealing how common those difficulties are. Provisional costs establish that staffing, security and operations belong in the decision, but not the precise scale of the financial consequences.

These sources therefore create a genuine policy tension. Additional availability may address access needs, yet its benefits and resource requirements cannot be measured confidently from the supplied information. Maintaining current arrangements avoids committing to a change, but that choice must still be considered against possible unmet needs. Targeting extensions changes the relationship between availability and anticipated demand, but deciding when demand will be greatest remains uncertain.

How to make the judgement

Begin with your priorities. If access and equity receive greatest weight, explain why those considerations matter and how the evidence bears on them. If resource restraint or uncertainty receives greater weight, explain why that deserves priority instead. Neither approach is automatically superior: its strength depends on the connection between the criterion, the evidence and the policy consequences.

Then compare your selected action with both alternatives. A defensible argument recognises what the rejected policies might achieve, identifies the trade-offs your own choice accepts, and explains why your priorities justify accepting them. The argument is weaker if it treats the survey as representative of all students, assumes previous occupancy predicts future demand, invents precise costs or dismisses competing policies without examination.

Finally, make a clear provisional judgement. The decision can be reasoned and actionable without being certain. Your conclusion should follow from the relative weight you give the evidence, access needs, resource commitments and unresolved uncertainty. Another learner may reasonably choose differently while meeting the same standard of justification.

Assumptions, Uncertainty and Confidence

What makes a decision provisional?

A provisional decision is a reasoned choice made using the evidence currently available, with an acknowledgement that important questions remain unresolved. Provisional does not mean arbitrary or unjustified. It means the reasoning is open to reconsideration if relevant information changes.

Distinguish three elements of your reasoning

Observation: Something recorded or explicitly supplied in the case, such as a survey count or a description of previous library use.

Inference: An interpretation or anticipated consequence drawn from an observation. An inference may be reasonable without being certain.

Assumption: A condition your reasoning depends on but which the available evidence does not establish. An assumption becomes consequential when your preference or confidence would change materially if it proved incorrect.

For example, a decision about opening hours may depend on expectations about how students will use additional availability. The original survey, historical records and access comments offer relevant information, but they do not establish every future consequence. Provisional costs create further uncertainty about the resources different actions may require.

Calibrate confidence

Confidence is not the same as certainty that a policy will succeed. It is your judgement about how strongly the available evidence and comparative reasoning support your selected action.

Confidence may be strengthened when the relevant evidence is consistent with the recommendation, the reasoning addresses both alternatives, and important trade-offs have been examined. It may be limited when the decision depends heavily on unverified assumptions, evidence that cannot be generalised confidently, or operational implications that remain provisional.

You may express confidence in words, such as a carefully qualified level, provided you explain what that level means in relation to your reasoning. There is no prescribed confidence level for any of the three options.

A transparent recommendation can therefore state a clear action, acknowledge uncertainty and identify the assumptions on which the decision depends. This makes the original reasoning available for meaningful reconsideration later.

Confidence without an evidential rationale can make a provisional judgement sound either unjustifiably certain or arbitrarily hesitant.

What to look for

  • A. A recognisable degree of confidence is stated.
  • B. The confidence judgement refers to both supporting grounds and significant limitations.
  • C. A consequential development that could change confidence is identified.

If something is missing

  • Missing A: Describe how strongly you currently support your selected action rather than leaving confidence implicit.
  • Missing B: Connect your confidence to the strongest evidence-based reason for your choice and to an uncertainty that limits it.
  • Missing C: Specify which unresolved assumption or evidence gap would materially affect your confidence if resolved differently.

Criterion 4 of 4

Does your briefing communicate the same provisional judgement clearly to someone with different priorities?

Why this matters

A decision briefing must make its reasoning inspectable without assuming the audience already knows the case or shares the decision-maker's values.

What to look for

  • A. The briefing identifies the same selected option as the original recommendation.
  • B. The strongest supporting reason is linked to original evidence and remains appropriately qualified.
  • C. The briefing acknowledges a meaningful trade-off or assumption and explains confidence.

If something is missing

  • Missing A: Check that the option named in the briefing matches your original decision rather than introducing a revised choice.
  • Missing B: Name the relevant original evidence detail and explain its significance without turning it into a population-wide claim or certain forecast.
  • Missing C: Add the compromise or unverified condition a differently prioritising colleague should know, then connect your confidence level to that limitation.

Transfer your learning

Brief a Colleague with Different Priorities

A university colleague who has not participated in these learning activities asks you to explain your provisional library opening-hours decision. The colleague may place a different emphasis on student access and operational resources.

Write a concise decision briefing using your existing recommendation. Identify the selected option, its strongest evidence-based reason, one important trade-off or assumption, and your justified degree of confidence.

Your task is to communicate the reasoning clearly to someone who may not share your priorities. Do not invent new evidence, change the three available policies or assume the colleague will agree with your conclusion.

Complete the associated briefing workspace.

Your draft is saved on this device when possible. It is not submitted.

Study tips

  • Evidence to comparison: Use original observations accurately, recognise their limitations and compare the consequences of all three available actions against shared criteria.
  • Comparison to judgement: Make your priorities explicit, explain why one provisional action is preferable to both alternatives, and acknowledge the trade-offs you accept.
  • Judgement under uncertainty: Distinguish evidence from assumptions, identify unresolved questions and express confidence in proportion to the strength of your reasoning.
  • Retain your reasoning: Keep an accessible copy of the original recommendation, justification, assumptions and confidence so that you can reconsider it meaningfully in c4.
