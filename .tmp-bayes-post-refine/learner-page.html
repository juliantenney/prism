Bayes' theorem: how evidence changes probability

What should happen to a probability when new evidence arrives?

Suppose you initially think an explanation is unlikely. Then you encounter some new evidence that would be unusual unless that explanation were true. It is reasonable for your confidence in the explanation to increase. If, instead, the evidence would be just as common whether the explanation were true or false, it should change your confidence much less.

Bayes' theorem gives a precise form to this familiar kind of reasoning. It starts with a probability before the new evidence is taken into account and provides a way to revise that probability in the light of the evidence. The starting probability is called the prior probability. The revised probability is called the posterior probability.

The change from prior to posterior is an update rather than a declaration of certainty. A possibility might begin with a probability of 1% and, after strong evidence, rise substantially while still remaining below 50%. Saying that evidence has made something much more plausible is therefore different from saying that it has become certain, or even that it has become more likely than not.

This prior-to-posterior movement is the organising idea behind Bayes' theorem. To understand how the update works, however, we first need to be precise about the direction in which probabilities are being stated.

The crucial distinction: which probability are we asking for?

A conditional probability is a probability evaluated under a stated condition. The notation P(A|B) means 'the probability of A given B'. The vertical bar can be read as 'given that'.

The order matters. P(A|B) and P(B|A) refer to different questions because they start from different groups. Consider a university example. The probability that someone is a university employee given that they are a professor may be very high: we begin with professors and ask how many are university employees. The probability that someone is a professor given that they are a university employee may be much lower: now we begin with all university employees and ask how many are professors. The same two categories appear in both statements, but the reference group has changed.

This becomes especially consequential when interpreting evidence. Suppose a test gives a positive result to 90% of people who have a particular condition. This tells us P(positive|condition): if we begin with people who have the condition, 90% receive a positive result. It does not tell us P(condition|positive): if we begin with everyone who received a positive result, what proportion actually have the condition?

These two probabilities can be very different. The second question depends not only on what happens when the condition is present, but also on how common the condition was in the first place and how often positive results occur when it is absent. Bayes' theorem provides the relationship needed to move from one direction of conditioning to the other.

What Bayes' theorem combines

We can now state Bayes' theorem using a hypothesis H and some observed evidence E:

P(H|E) = [P(E|H) × P(H)] / P(E)

Each part corresponds to a different element of the reasoning.

P(H) is the prior probability: how probable H was before taking the new evidence into account. P(E|H) is the probability of observing the evidence if H is true. P(H|E) is the posterior probability: how probable H is after the evidence has been incorporated.

The denominator, P(E), represents the overall probability of observing the evidence. This term is easier to understand if we ask where the evidence could have come from. Evidence may occur when H is true, but it may also occur when H is false or when another explanation applies. P(E) has to account for these relevant routes to the same observation.

For a test result, for example, a positive result can arise because someone has the condition and the test correctly returns positive. It can also arise because someone does not have the condition but the test nevertheless returns positive. Looking only at the first route would ignore some of the positive results we are trying to interpret.

Bayes' theorem therefore combines two kinds of information that are easy to separate in ordinary reasoning: what was plausible before the evidence arrived, and how the observed evidence is distributed across the possible explanations. The formula is a compact expression of that relationship rather than a replacement for it.

P(H|E) = [P(E|H) × P(H)] / P(E)

  • Prior probability — How probable the hypothesis was before taking the new evidence into account.
  • Probability of the evidence if the hypothesis is true — How probable the observed evidence would be if the hypothesis were true.
  • Overall probability of the evidence — How probable the evidence is across the relevant ways in which it could arise.
  • Posterior probability — How probable the hypothesis is after the evidence has been incorporated.

Making the update visible: a rare condition and a positive test

Consider a hypothetical condition affecting 1% of a population. A test gives a positive result to 90% of people who have the condition and to 5% of people who do not.

It is tempting to focus on the 90% figure. But that is P(positive|condition), while the question we usually want to answer after seeing a positive result is P(condition|positive). Natural frequencies make the difference visible.

Imagine 10,000 people. Because the condition affects 1%, about 100 people have it and 9,900 do not. Of the 100 people with the condition, 90% test positive, giving about 90 positive results. Of the 9,900 people without the condition, 5% test positive, giving about 495 positive results.

There are therefore about 585 positive results altogether: 90 from people with the condition and 495 from people without it. If we now begin with the group that tested positive, the proportion who actually have the condition is 90 out of 585, or approximately 15.4%.

The result is striking because 90% and 15.4% answer different conditional questions. Ninety per cent describes what happens when we begin with people who have the condition. Approximately 15.4% describes what happens when we begin with people who have tested positive.

The same reasoning can be expressed through Bayes' theorem. Let H mean that the person has the condition and E mean that the test is positive. The prior probability is P(H) = 0.01. The probability of a positive result if the condition is present is P(E|H) = 0.90.

The overall probability of a positive result must include both routes by which a positive can occur. Positive results from people with the condition contribute 0.90 × 0.01 = 0.009. Positive results from people without the condition contribute 0.05 × 0.99 = 0.0495. Therefore P(E) = 0.009 + 0.0495 = 0.0585.

Substituting these values gives P(H|E) = (0.90 × 0.01) / 0.0585 ≈ 0.154, the same approximately 15.4% obtained from the natural-frequency representation.

The two approaches are not separate methods. The formula and the imagined population describe the same structure. The population representation makes visible what the denominator is doing: it includes all the positive results that need to be considered, not just those associated with the condition.

Why context changes what the same evidence means

The approximately 15.4% posterior in the worked example is not an intrinsic property of a positive result. It depends partly on the population in which the test is being used.

Keep the test unchanged: it still returns positive for 90% of people with the condition and 5% of people without it. Now suppose the condition occurs in 20% of the relevant population rather than 1%.

Among 1,000 people, about 200 would have the condition. Of these, 180 would test positive. The remaining 800 people would not have the condition, and about 40 of them would nevertheless test positive. There would therefore be 220 positive results, of which 180 would come from people with the condition. The posterior probability after a positive result is now about 180/220, or 81.8%.

Nothing about the test itself has changed. The probability of a positive result when the condition is present remains 90%, and the probability of a positive result when it is absent remains 5%. What changed was the prior probability: the condition was much more common in the second population.

The contrast explains why base rates cannot simply be discarded once evidence arrives. With a 1% prior, the large population without the condition creates many opportunities for false positives. With a 20% prior, true positives make up a much larger proportion of all positive results.

A piece of evidence therefore does not carry one fixed posterior meaning into every context. Its interpretation depends partly on what was plausible before it was observed.

When is evidence actually informative?

The diagnostic example reveals a broader principle. Evidence is informative not merely because it is compatible with a hypothesis, but because of how its probability under that hypothesis compares with its probability under alternatives.

Suppose a university is investigating a fall in engagement with an optional online resource. One possible explanation is that students are having difficulty finding the resource in the virtual learning environment. Analytics show that many affected students reach the module homepage but do not open the resource.

That observation is compatible with the navigation explanation: students who cannot find the resource may well reach the homepage without opening it. Compatibility alone, however, is not enough. The same pattern could also occur when students can find the resource but decide not to use it.

If reaching the homepage without opening the resource is common under both explanations, the observation does relatively little to distinguish between them. If the pattern is much more likely when students cannot locate the resource than when they can locate it, the evidence provides stronger support for the navigation explanation.

This is the same structure encountered in the test example. A positive test is not interpreted only by asking how often positive results occur when the condition is present. We also consider how often they occur when it is absent. In both cases, the evidential force comes from comparison across possible explanations.

This way of reasoning guards against a common overstatement: 'the evidence fits my explanation, therefore my explanation is established'. Several explanations may fit the same observation. Bayesian reasoning asks how the observation redistributes probability among those possibilities, given what was plausible beforehand and how readily each possibility could have produced the evidence.

Bayesian updating can continue as further evidence becomes available. Once a prior has been combined with evidence, the resulting posterior can serve as the prior for a later update. Conceptually, the sequence is straightforward: prior probability, evidence, posterior probability; then further evidence and another revised posterior.

This makes Bayesian reasoning suitable for situations in which information accumulates over time. An explanation need not be accepted or rejected in a single step. Its probability can change as relevant observations are incorporated.

Repeated updating does not mean that every new observation can simply be treated as an independent piece of support. Pieces of evidence may be related. If two observations largely reflect the same underlying information, treating them as though they were independent can exaggerate their combined evidential force. The relationships among the evidence therefore form part of the interpretation.

The result is also only as well grounded as the quantities and assumptions supplied to the reasoning. Bayes' theorem specifies a mathematical relationship, but it does not automatically decide whether a prior probability is well justified, whether the probabilities assigned to the evidence represent the situation accurately, or whether the relevant alternative explanations have been adequately considered.

Nor does a posterior probability turn uncertainty into certainty. In the diagnostic example, the probability rose from 1% to about 15.4% after a positive result. That is a substantial update: the condition became much more plausible than it was before. Yet, under the assumptions of the example, it remained more likely that a person with a positive result did not have the condition. 'Much more plausible than before' and 'more likely than not' are different claims.

The reasoning developed across these examples can therefore be carried without treating Bayes' theorem as merely a formula to remember. Begin with what was plausible before the new evidence. Ask how likely the observed evidence would be if the hypothesis were true. Consider how readily the same evidence could arise through relevant alternatives. Use those relationships to revise the probability, and interpret the resulting posterior as conditional on the evidence and assumptions that produced it.

Bayesian reasoning is, in this sense, disciplined revision under uncertainty. Evidence can and should change what we regard as plausible, but its force depends on prior context and on what else could have produced what we observed. The posterior captures that revised state of uncertainty; it does not abolish uncertainty itself.
