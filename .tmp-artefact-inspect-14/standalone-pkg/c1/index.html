Evidence, Interpretation and Uncertainty

A decision without a complete answer

Imagine that your university is deciding whether to change its library opening hours during the examination period. Students need somewhere to study, but keeping a building open for longer requires staff, security and other resources. The university wants to make a reasonable decision, yet it cannot know in advance exactly how students would respond to different opening hours.

There are three options under consideration:

  • Option A: Maintain existing opening hours.
  • Option B: Extend opening hours throughout the examination period.
  • Option C: Introduce targeted extensions during periods of greatest anticipated demand.

Each option raises questions. How many students want additional opening hours? Would they actually use them? Do some students have fewer opportunities than others to study at home? What would additional opening hours cost?

The university has some relevant information. Students have responded to a survey about opening hours. Building-access records show how the library was used during a previous examination period. Some students have described difficulties finding suitable places to study. The university also knows that extending hours would involve additional expenditure.

These sources are useful, but they do not provide a complete answer. A survey records what respondents said, not necessarily what every student thinks. Previous library use does not tell us exactly what would happen under different opening hours. Individual experiences may reveal important difficulties without showing how widespread they are. Costs depend partly on the arrangements eventually chosen.

The challenge is not simply to collect information and read off the correct option. Someone must decide what the information establishes, what it might suggest and what remains unknown. Those distinctions are the foundation of evidence-informed judgement.

Evidence, interpretation and assumption: Three different roles

Consider three statements about the university's proposed library opening hours:

  1. A voluntary survey received 240 responses, and 168 respondents supported extended opening hours.
  2. The survey suggests that longer opening hours could benefit some students.
  3. Students who support longer opening hours will use the library during those additional hours.

These statements are connected, but they do different things.

Evidence: What information do we have?

Evidence is information relevant to supporting or challenging a claim. A claim is something that can be considered in light of available information. Evidence might include observations, records, survey responses or accounts of people's experiences.

The first statement reports a survey finding. It tells us what was recorded: 240 people responded, and 168 of them supported extending opening hours. Assuming the survey figures have been reported accurately, this is evidence about the preferences expressed by those respondents.

Notice the limits of the statement. It does not say that all students support longer opening hours. It does not say that respondents would use the additional hours. It simply reports the available information.

Interpretation: What might the evidence mean?

An interpretation is an explanation of what evidence might indicate. It moves beyond reporting information to considering its significance.

The second statement interprets the survey result. If many respondents favour longer opening hours, it is reasonable to consider whether additional access could benefit some students. However, the survey does not directly measure the benefits students would receive. The interpretation connects the recorded preferences to a possible consequence.

An interpretation is not automatically wrong because it goes beyond the evidence. Reasoning often requires us to consider what information means. The question is whether the interpretation is plausible and whether its limitations are acknowledged.

Assumption: What are we treating as true?

An assumption is something treated as true without sufficient evidence establishing it in the situation.

The third statement assumes that students who express support will actually use the additional hours. That might happen, but the survey result alone does not establish it. Someone might favour longer opening hours because they value having the option, even if they rarely use it. Others might use the library more often than they initially expect.

Assumptions are not necessarily false. Sometimes decisions require assumptions about circumstances that cannot yet be observed. What matters is recognising them rather than presenting them as findings.

How the three roles connect

A line of reasoning might begin with a reported finding, develop an interpretation and rely on an assumption. For example:

  • Evidence: 168 of 240 survey respondents supported extended hours.
  • Interpretation: Longer opening hours may meet a need among some students.
  • Assumption: Those students would make use of the additional opening hours.

The first statement reports information. The second proposes what it might mean. The third introduces something that has not been established.

These are roles within reasoning, rather than permanent labels attached to sentences. If the university later collected reliable information about actual use of extended hours, a proposition previously treated as an assumption might become supported by evidence.

Keeping the roles distinct makes an argument easier to examine. It allows you to agree that a finding is accurate while questioning an interpretation, or to accept that an interpretation is plausible while recognising that it depends on an uncertain assumption.

When evidence supports a claim but does not prove it

The university's survey offers a useful example of why evidence can support a claim without proving it.

The university has approximately 12,000 students. A voluntary online survey about library opening hours received 240 responses. Of those respondents, 168 supported extended opening hours.

That means 70% of respondents supported an extension. It does not mean that 70% of all students supported one.

The difference comes from the group being described. The survey result directly concerns the 240 people who chose to respond. The university's wider student population is much larger, and the survey does not establish that respondents reflect the views of everyone else.

From a finding to a broader claim

Consider three statements:

  • '168 of the 240 respondents supported extended hours.' This reports the survey finding.
  • 'The survey provides evidence that some students favour longer opening hours.' This is supported by the finding, while remaining appropriately limited.
  • 'Seventy per cent of all university students want longer opening hours.' This goes beyond what the survey establishes.

The third statement might turn out to be true, but the available survey cannot establish it. The problem is not the arithmetic. Seventy per cent is the correct proportion of respondents who supported an extension. The problem is applying that percentage to a different group without sufficient justification.

Why voluntary participation matters

The survey was voluntary: students decided whether to respond. People with strong opinions about library opening hours may have been particularly motivated to participate. Those who were less interested might have been less likely to respond.

This creates uncertainty about representativeness: how far the respondents reflect the wider student population.

We cannot tell from the supplied information how closely the survey represents all students. We also cannot calculate the preferences of non-respondents. The survey therefore gives useful evidence of expressed support among respondents, but it does not settle the preferences of the whole university.

Support is not the same as proof

Evidence supports a claim when it provides reasons to regard the claim as credible. Evidence proves a claim only when it is sufficient to establish it conclusively. Many practical decisions rely on evidence that provides support without removing all uncertainty.

The survey supports the claim that there is interest in longer opening hours among at least some students. It does not prove that most students want them, that respondents will use the additional hours, or that extending hours is the best option.

This does not make the survey worthless. Rejecting the broader generalisation does not require rejecting the narrower finding. A careful conclusion preserves what the evidence establishes while avoiding claims it cannot justify.

For example: 'A majority of survey respondents supported extended hours, suggesting that additional access may be valued by some students. Because participation was voluntary, the result does not establish the preferences of the wider student population.'

That conclusion is qualified, but it is not indecisive. It tells us both what the survey contributes and what remains uncertain.

What remains uncertain, and why?

The survey is not the only source of information available to the university. It also has records of previous library use and comments from students about their study circumstances. These sources reveal different kinds of uncertainty.

Previous use is not the same as future demand

Building-access records from a previous examination period show that late-evening occupancy was relatively low compared with daytime use.

That is a finding about observed behaviour: how the building was used under the arrangements operating at the time.

One interpretation is that relatively few students need library access late in the evening. If that interpretation is accurate, extending opening hours might produce limited additional use.

But another possibility is that existing arrangements restricted opportunities to study later. Students cannot use opening hours that are not available, and their behaviour might change if the university offered different access.

The records do not establish which interpretation best predicts future use. They tell us what happened previously, not exactly what would happen under a new arrangement.

This distinction separates historical usage from future demand. Historical records are relevant evidence, but predictions about future demand require an additional step in reasoning. That step may depend on assumptions about how students would respond to changed opening hours.

The appropriate conclusion is not that the records prove there is no need for an extension. Nor is it that the records are irrelevant. They provide useful information about previous occupancy while leaving demand for newly available hours uncertain.

Reported difficulties are not the same as their prevalence

The university has also received a small collection of student comments describing difficulties studying at home. Some students mention shared accommodation, caring responsibilities or limited access to quiet space.

These comments provide evidence of reported experiences. They identify circumstances in which access to a university study space may be particularly valuable.

However, the comments do not tell us how widespread those circumstances are across the university. The number of comments is small, and no information has been provided that would establish the proportion of students affected.

This is the distinction between an experience being reported and its prevalence: how common it is in a wider population.

It would be unsupported to conclude that most students lack suitable study space at home. It would also be mistaken to dismiss the comments simply because their prevalence is unknown. The reports identify possible access difficulties that are relevant to the decision, even though they do not establish how many students experience them.

Different evidence leaves different questions open

The survey tells us about the preferences of respondents, but not necessarily the whole population. Historical access records tell us about previous occupancy, but not demand for newly available hours. Student comments describe reported difficulties, but not how widespread they are.

Each source has a different contribution and a different limitation.

Uncertainty means that some relevant questions cannot be answered confidently or completely from the available information. It does not mean that nothing is known.

A useful way to express a conclusion is to state what the evidence establishes and then identify the unresolved question. For example: 'Previous records show relatively low late-evening occupancy, but they do not establish how students would use newly available opening hours.'

That statement preserves the finding and its limitation together. It avoids turning uncertainty into either unwarranted certainty or a reason to ignore the evidence.

Making a reasonable judgement when information is incomplete

From available evidence to provisional judgement

The university now has several pieces of relevant information, but important questions remain unanswered.

The survey shows support for extended opening hours among respondents, although it does not establish the preferences of all students. Previous access records show relatively low late-evening occupancy compared with daytime use, although they cannot establish demand for newly available hours. Student comments identify difficulties studying at home, although they do not show how widespread those difficulties are.

The university must also consider resources. Extending opening hours would require additional staffing, security and operational expenditure. The costs are provisional because they depend on how far and when opening hours are extended.

None of these findings, considered alone, establishes what the university should do.

Considering the three alternatives

Option A: Maintain existing opening hours. This would avoid expenditure associated with extending opening hours. However, maintaining the existing arrangements might leave some students' requests or access difficulties unresolved. The evidence does not establish how many students would be affected.

Option B: Extend opening hours throughout the examination period. This would make additional library access available during the period. Some students might value or use that access, but the survey does not establish how many would use it. Additional staffing, security and operational costs would also need to be considered.

Option C: Introduce targeted extensions during periods of greatest anticipated demand. This would concentrate additional opening hours at selected times. Its usefulness would depend partly on whether the university could anticipate demand accurately. Historical records may inform that judgement, but they cannot establish future demand for newly available hours.

These are considerations, not settled conclusions about which option is best. Different interpretations of the available evidence could lead people to place different weight on access, anticipated use and costs.

What makes a judgement provisional?

A provisional judgement is a reasoned conclusion made using the information currently available while recognising that the conclusion may need to change if further evidence becomes available.

Provisional does not mean random, careless or unsupported. It means that the reasons for a judgement are made clear without pretending that all uncertainty has disappeared.

A transparent explanation of a decision would distinguish four things:

  1. What the evidence establishes. For example, the survey records support among 168 respondents, and previous records show relatively low late-evening occupancy compared with daytime use.
  2. What is interpreted from that evidence. For example, survey support may suggest that additional access would benefit some students, while historical usage may suggest limits to likely demand.
  3. What is being assumed. For example, a prediction about how students would use additional opening hours depends on assumptions about future behaviour.
  4. What remains uncertain. For example, the university does not know the preferences of all students, the demand for newly available hours, the prevalence of reported study difficulties or the final costs of an extension.

These distinctions allow a judgement to be examined and, if necessary, reconsidered. New information might strengthen an existing interpretation, weaken an assumption or change the apparent merits of an alternative.

Reasoning without pretending to know everything

A university may need to decide on opening hours before every question can be answered. Waiting for complete certainty may not be possible, but acting without examining the evidence would also be difficult to justify.

Evidence-informed judgement lies between those extremes. It uses relevant information, explains what that information supports, acknowledges the assumptions connecting findings to possible consequences, and recognises unresolved questions.

The library case remains open: Option A, Option B and Option C each involve considerations that the available evidence does not settle conclusively. The purpose of distinguishing evidence, interpretation and assumption is not to make every decision obvious. It is to make the reasoning behind a decision clearer.

You can now recognise the different roles within an argument. A finding describes what is recorded. An interpretation explains what that finding might mean. An assumption identifies something being treated as true without sufficient evidence. Uncertainty marks the limits of what can be established, and a provisional judgement uses the available information without concealing those limits.

A conclusion can therefore be careful and useful without being final. Evidence may support a claim without proving it, and a reasonable decision may still be necessary while important questions remain unanswered.
