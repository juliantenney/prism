Your recommendation: traffic management near a school

School-area traffic-management decision: information available to the local authority

Scenario

The local authority is considering a temporary response to vehicle activity near a school during arrival and departure periods. Its aim is to make a defensible decision while recognising potential pedestrian-safety concerns, practical constraints and uncertainty about the consequences of intervention. The following information is fictional and represents the evidence currently available to the authority.

Alternatives

  1. Maintain existing traffic arrangements

    Continue with the current arrangements without introducing a new traffic-management measure. This avoids the immediate implementation costs and disruption associated with a change, but existing traffic conditions and reported concerns would remain unaddressed by a new intervention.

  2. Temporary vehicle-access restriction

    Introduce a temporary restriction on vehicle access near the school during arrival and departure periods. This could reduce vehicle activity immediately outside the school, but implementation would involve costs and disruption, and some traffic might move to surrounding roads.

  3. Additional signage and targeted traffic management

    Introduce a more limited intervention using additional signage and targeted traffic-management measures. This may be easier to implement and less disruptive than a temporary access restriction, but its effect on vehicle activity and pedestrian-safety concerns is uncertain.

Evidence

Traffic observations

Observations undertaken over a short period recorded substantial vehicle activity near the school during arrival and departure times.

Qualification: The observation period was limited. It is not known whether the observed conditions are representative of other days or periods.

Parent and resident reports

Some parents and local residents have reported congestion and concerns about pedestrian safety near the school.

Qualification: These reports provide evidence of experiences and perceived risks. They do not independently establish how frequently dangerous incidents occur.

Recorded collisions

Formal records show few reported collisions in the immediate area.

Qualification: The records provide information about documented collisions but do not establish that pedestrian risk is absent.

Possible effects of restricting access

A temporary access restriction could reduce vehicle activity immediately outside the school during the periods when it operates.

Qualification: The scale of any reduction has not been established. Some vehicles may use surrounding roads instead, and the extent or consequences of this displacement are uncertain.

Costs, disruption and implementation

The three options differ in their likely implementation demands. Maintaining existing arrangements avoids the immediate cost and disruption of a new measure. Temporary access restrictions are likely to require more substantial implementation arrangements and cause greater disruption than the more limited signage and targeted-management option.

Qualification: These comparisons are provisional. Precise costs, implementation requirements and the effectiveness of either intervention have not been established.

The supplied information is sufficient to support reasoned but provisional recommendations. It does not establish that any single option is necessarily best. The authority has no additional verified evidence available for this decision.

Review your evidence-informed judgement

Your recommendation is not judged against a single correct traffic-management option. What matters is how convincingly your reasoning connects the available evidence to your decision. Read your completed response alongside the criteria below. For each criterion, identify a passage that demonstrates the capability, or recognise where your explanation could be stronger. This is a self-evaluation aid, not an automatically generated assessment of your work.

Evidence recognition and evaluation

Consider whether your response distinguishes what the supplied information directly establishes from what is inferred or assumed. The short-period traffic observations establish substantial vehicle activity during the observed times, but not how typical those conditions are. Parent and resident reports establish that concerns have been expressed, but do not verify the frequency of dangerous incidents. Few recorded collisions do not prove that pedestrian risk is absent. Strong reasoning recognises what these sources contribute without overstating their implications or dismissing apparently conflicting information.

Comparison and trade-offs

Consider whether you have compared all three available alternatives rather than concentrating only on your preferred option. Maintaining existing arrangements avoids immediate intervention costs and disruption but does not introduce a new response to the reported concerns. Temporary access restrictions could reduce traffic immediately outside the school, but their effects, disruption and possible displacement remain uncertain. Additional signage and targeted traffic management may be easier to implement and less disruptive, but their effectiveness is also uncertain. A meaningful comparison explains why particular benefits, disadvantages and trade-offs matter to your decision.

Recommendation and justification

Consider whether your recommendation is clear and whether your reasons explain why it is preferable to the other two options on the available evidence. A defensible judgement does more than list supporting points: it connects evidence, its limitations and the relative merits of the alternatives to an explained preference. Options A, B and C can each support a defensible provisional recommendation. The quality of the reasoning matters more than the option selected.

Uncertainty and reconsideration

Consider whether you have identified uncertainties that could materially affect your decision rather than merely stating that more information is needed. Relevant uncertainties include whether the traffic observations are representative, the extent of pedestrian-safety risks, the effects of restrictions on surrounding roads, and the costs or effectiveness of possible interventions. Strong reasoning explains what additional information could strengthen, qualify or overturn the recommendation and why that information would matter. A provisional decision can be justified without pretending that all uncertainty has been resolved.

Independent transfer

Consider whether your response demonstrates that you can apply evidence-informed reasoning to this unfamiliar school traffic-management decision. The relevant capability is the independent use of evidence recognition, evaluation, comparison, justification and openness to reconsideration. You do not need to reproduce an earlier university library decision or use earlier responses. Your completed judgement is the evidence of how you applied those capabilities in this new context.

If you wish, revisit your response after reviewing the criteria. You might clarify an inference, strengthen a comparison, explain a trade-off more fully or identify more consequential conditions for reconsideration. Keep your original response as well as any revised version so you can distinguish your initial judgement from subsequent reflection. Revision is optional and does not affect a grade or progression decision.

Return to your response

Keep an accessible copy of your completed traffic-management judgement, including your original recommendation and reasoning. In c6, you will use that response to examine the strengths and limitations of your independent evidence-informed judgement. You may use the criteria above to notice areas you would now explain differently, but your original response remains useful evidence of your thinking at the time.
