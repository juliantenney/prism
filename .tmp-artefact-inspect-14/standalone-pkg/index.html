Deciding Under Uncertainty

Making a decision when the evidence is incomplete

Important decisions rarely come with perfect information. Evidence may be incomplete, sources may disagree, and different people may draw different conclusions from the same facts. Yet a decision may still be necessary.

In this Learning Journey, you will practise moving from uncertain evidence to a decision you can explain and defend. You will learn to distinguish what the evidence establishes from what people infer or assume, evaluate competing information, compare possible actions and justify a provisional choice.

You will also revisit an earlier decision when new evidence emerges. The aim is not always to change your mind, but to recognise when maintaining, qualifying or revising a judgement is justified.

You will begin with a university decision about library opening hours. As the journey develops, you will take increasing responsibility for evaluating evidence and making a recommendation. Finally, you will apply the same reasoning independently to an unfamiliar decision about traffic management near a school.

What you will be able to do

By the end, you should be able to:

- Distinguish evidence from interpretations and assumptions.
- Evaluate the relevance and limitations of competing evidence.
- Compare plausible actions and defend a provisional decision.
- Reconsider a judgement when new information becomes available.
- Apply these capabilities independently in an unfamiliar situation.

How to study

Allow approximately 120 minutes in total, completed at your own pace over one week. The six experiences build on one another, so work through them in order. You may take breaks between experiences.

You will sometimes need to retain your own reasoning for use later. In particular, keep a copy of the decision and justification you produce in the third experience. You will return to it when new evidence becomes available.

There may be more than one defensible answer. The quality of your reasoning matters more than selecting a particular option.

  1. 1

    What do we actually know?

    A university is considering whether to change its library opening hours during an examination period. Students have expressed different needs, usage information is incomplete, and extending access would involve additional costs.

    Before deciding what the university should do, you need to establish what the available information actually tells you.

    In this experience, you will explore the difference between evidence, interpretation and assumption. You will see why a plausible claim is not necessarily an established fact, and why uncertainty does not always prevent a reasonable decision.

    Allow approximately 15 minutes.

    Open this experience

  2. 2

    Which evidence deserves weight?

    You now know why evidence and interpretation must be distinguished. The next challenge is deciding how much confidence to place in different sources.

    You will examine information about student preferences, previous library usage, access to alternative study spaces and the costs of extended opening hours.

    Some evidence is relevant but limited. Some sources appear to point in different directions because they answer different questions. You will evaluate what each source can reasonably support and compare its significance for the university's decision.

    You are not yet choosing an action. Your task is to establish which evidence matters, why it matters and what remains uncertain.

    Allow approximately 25 minutes. Keep your main conclusions in mind for the next experience.

    Open this experience

  3. 3

    From evidence to a defensible decision

    Evaluating evidence is important, but decisions require you to go further: you must decide what should be done.

    The university has three possible actions:

    - A: Maintain existing library opening hours.
    - B: Extend opening hours throughout the examination period.
    - C: Introduce targeted extensions during periods of greatest anticipated demand.

    You will compare these alternatives using the evidence you have examined. Consider likely benefits, costs, access needs and uncertainties. Then choose an action and explain why it is preferable to the alternatives.

    You do not need to eliminate uncertainty or discover a single correct answer. You do need to make your reasoning visible.

    Allow approximately 30 minutes. Keep a copy of your chosen action and justification. You will need your original reasoning in the next experience.

    Open this experience

  4. 4

    Would new evidence change your mind?

    A defensible decision is not necessarily a final decision. New information may strengthen its justification, weaken it or change which action appears preferable.

    Return to the library decision you made previously. You will encounter additional evidence about when students need access, who may benefit most and the relative costs of different opening arrangements.

    Your task is to reconsider your own earlier judgement. Does the new evidence justify maintaining your decision, qualifying your confidence or choosing a different action?

    Changing your mind is not automatically better than staying with your original recommendation. What matters is whether your response follows from the evidence.

    Allow approximately 20 minutes. Have your earlier decision and justification available before beginning.

    Open this experience

  5. 5

    Make the judgement yourself

    You have practised distinguishing evidence from assumptions, evaluating competing information, choosing an action and reconsidering it when circumstances change.

    Now you will apply those capabilities independently in a different setting.

    A local authority is considering whether to introduce a temporary traffic-management measure near a school. You will receive evidence about traffic activity, safety concerns, recorded incidents, possible consequences and implementation costs.

    You must decide which action is best justified and explain your reasoning. Consider the strengths and limitations of the evidence, compare plausible alternatives and identify what additional information might change your judgement.

    This is an opportunity to demonstrate independent, evidence-informed judgement. The task does not require specialist knowledge or external research, and more than one recommendation may be defensible.

    Allow approximately 25 minutes. Complete and retain your response before moving to the final experience.

    Open this experience

  6. 6

    How strong was your reasoning?

    Return to the decision you made about traffic management near the school.

    You will review your own response against the qualities of a defensible judgement: appropriate use of evidence, recognition of limitations, comparison of alternatives, clear justification, proportionate uncertainty and openness to reconsideration.

    Identify one aspect of your reasoning that was strong and one that could be improved.

    The purpose is not to check whether you selected a preferred answer. It is to recognise the reasoning habits you can use when making decisions in other subjects, professional situations and everyday life.

    Allow approximately 5 minutes.

    Open this experience
