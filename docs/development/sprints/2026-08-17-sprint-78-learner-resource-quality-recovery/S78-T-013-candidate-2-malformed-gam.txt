          "title": "Objectives and Constraints in Optimisation",
          "body_format": "markdown",
          "body": "In a constrained optimisation problem, economists seek the best achievable outcome while recognising that choices are limited by scarce resources. The system has two connected parts. The objective function specifies what is being optimised, such as utility, profit, or output. The constraint identifies the limitation that prevents every desired outcome from being simultaneously achieved.\n\nThe relationship between these parts is important. The objective defines success, while the constraint determines which options are feasible. An optimal choice must satisfy both. A consumer may wish to maximise utility, but available income limits the combinations of goods that can be purchased. A firm may wish to maximise output, but available labour or capital restricts production possibilities.\n\nWithout a constraint, optimisation focuses only on finding the highest or lowest value of the objective function. With a constraint, some desirable outcomes become unattainable because they exceed available resources. The problem therefore changes from finding the best possible outcome to finding the best feasible outcome.\n\nThinking about optimisation as a connected system helps explain why constraints matter. The objective function provides direction, the constraint provides boundaries, and the optimal solution lies where the best attainable outcome is achieved within those boundaries."

          "title": "What a Shadow Price Represents",
          "body_format": "markdown",
          "body": "A constrained optimisation problem contains limits that restrict the outcomes available to a decision-maker. When a constraint is binding, it prevents access to some combinations that would otherwise improve the objective being pursued.\n\nIf that constraint were relaxed slightly, additional feasible choices would become available. Because more options can now be considered, the best attainable value of the objective function may increase. The size of this improvement depends on how restrictive the original constraint was.\n\nThe Lagrange multiplier measures this marginal change. Rather than describing the objective function itself, it indicates how much the optimal value would change if the constraint were loosened by one unit. For this reason, economists interpret the multiplier as a shadow price.\n\nA larger multiplier means that relaxing the constraint creates a larger improvement in the attainable objective value. A smaller multiplier means that an additional unit of the constrained resource has less effect. The multiplier therefore conveys the value of relaxing the constraint and provides information about the economic significance of scarcity."
