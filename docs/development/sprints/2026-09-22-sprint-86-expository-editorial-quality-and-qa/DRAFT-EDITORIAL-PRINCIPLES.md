# Sprint 86 — Draft editorial principles (hypotheses)

**Status:** **DRAFT HYPOTHESES ONLY** — not accepted architecture, not a fixed rubric, not production rules.  
**Source:** Bayes' theorem Expository editorial review after Sprint 85.  
**Sprint:** [SPRINT-86-CHARTER.md](SPRINT-86-CHARTER.md) · Opening [S86-D01](decisions.md#s86-d01--open-sprint-86--expository-editorial-quality--qa)

Sprint 86 must test whether these principles are genuinely general, partly domain/resource-dependent, missing dimensions, overlapping, wrongly levelled, or insufficient to explain editorial mediocrity amid technical/pedagogical soundness.

**Bayes is evidence, not a template.** Do not conclude that every Expository Resource must open with a provocative question, contain a worked example, contain a comparison table, use a surprise statement, follow fixed rhetorical beats, or share one section structure.

---

## Draft principles (P1–P11)

1. **Begin by creating an intellectual need.**  
   Give the learner a reason to engage with the explanation rather than beginning primarily with administrative descriptions of what will be covered. The appropriate entry might be a problem, phenomenon, question, tension, surprising result, case, contrast or other subject-appropriate device.

2. **Make the intellectual journey perceptible without narrating it excessively.**  
   Progression should be experienced through the explanation rather than repeatedly announced through metacommentary.

3. **Give explanatory attention in proportion to intellectual difficulty.**  
   Extent should allocate attention to difficult distinctions, threshold concepts, counterintuitive results, important qualifications and other high-value explanatory needs rather than distributing prose evenly.

4. **Prefer development over repetition.**  
   Important ideas may recur, but recurrence should add qualification, formalisation, comparison, application, integration or other new understanding.

5. **Make representations perform explanatory work.**  
   Diagrams, equations, tables, examples and other artefacts should exist because they explain something particularly well, not merely because a visual or material seems desirable.

6. **Compose prose and representations as one explanation.**  
   Exposition should be able to move deliberately between prose, representation, example, abstraction, comparison and formalisation rather than treating supporting artefacts as supplementary decorations.

7. **Use rhetorical emphasis selectively.**  
   Important moments of contrast, surprise, synthesis, resolution or qualification may deserve stronger treatment; not every proposition should receive equal rhetorical weight.

8. **Trust the reader where understanding has already been established.**  
   Avoid unnecessary restatement, generic transitions and explanation of what the explanation has just explained. This is not a demand for terseness: additional words should contribute additional understanding.

9. **Expose learner value, not internal instructional machinery.**  
   PRISM may require Learning Outcomes, Knowledge Summary, planning artefacts and synthesis fields internally. Their existence does not imply that each should appear as a labelled learner-facing page region.

10. **Let typography reveal intellectual structure.**  
    Typography should support hierarchy, grouping, emphasis, reading rhythm and relationships between prose, mathematics and representations without drawing attention to itself.

11. **End by consolidating the developed understanding.**  
    Closure should show what the learner can now understand that they could not understand at the beginning, rather than mechanically summarising every section or producing multiple conclusions.

## Observed Bayes issues motivating the draft set

(Evidence for investigation — not automatic requirements.)

- excessive learner-facing front matter;
- repetition between Overview / Learning purpose / Knowledge summary / body / closing;
- overly regular generated prose rhythm;
- excessive explanatory metacommentary;
- claim → explanation → restatement patterns;
- insufficient rhetorical variation and emphasis;
- representations sometimes accompanying prose rather than participating;
- insufficient learner-facing hierarchy within sections;
- internal instructional artefacts exposed too directly as page furniture;
- closure occurring more than once;
- typography/rendering functional but not yet high-quality educational publishing level.
