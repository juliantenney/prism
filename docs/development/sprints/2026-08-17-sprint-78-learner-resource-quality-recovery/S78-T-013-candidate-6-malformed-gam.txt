          "body_format": "markdown",
          "body": "## Why optimisation needs constraints\n\nEconomic decision-makers often seek the best possible outcome, but they rarely have unlimited resources. A consumer may wish to maximise utility, a firm may wish to maximise profit, and a government may wish to maximise social welfare. These goals are represented by an objective function.\n\nThe challenge is that choices are restricted. A consumer faces a budget limit, a firm may have limited labour or capital, and a government operates within spending constraints. These restrictions are called constraints.\n\nThe interaction between the objective function and the constraint creates a constrained optimisation system. The objective function indicates what is being optimised, while the constraint determines which options are feasible. An optimal solution must satisfy both requirements: it must improve the objective as much as possible and remain within the allowable set of choices.\n\nWithout a constraint, optimisation searches across all possible choices. With a constraint, optimisation searches only within the feasible set defined by the restriction. The same objective can therefore produce a different optimum when a constraint is introduced.\n\nIn economics, constrained optimisation is valuable because most real-world decisions involve scarcity. Resources are limited, so the best solution is usually the best feasible solution rather than the theoretical best solution without limits."
